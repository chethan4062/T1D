Type 1, explained is an awareness project. It helps people understand type 1 diabetes. It does not replace a doctor, a diabetes care team or emergency services.

In an emergency

If someone is unconscious, having a seizure, cannot swallow, or you are unsure what to do, call your local medical emergency number straight away. Do not wait to read this site.

General information only

Everything on this site, including the Low helper, is general information for education and awareness. It is not medical advice, diagnosis or treatment, and using the site does not create a doctor–patient relationship with anyone.

Always ask a qualified health professional about your own health or the health of someone in your care. Never ignore professional advice, or delay getting it, because of something you read here.

Not reviewed by a medical professional

This site was written by people who are not doctors. Its facts were checked against published guidance from the organisations listed under Sources, but the site itself has not been reviewed or approved by a medical professional or by any of those organisations.

Advice differs between people and countries

Diabetes care is individual. Targets, doses and the right response to low blood sugar vary from person to person, are different for young children, and differ a little between countries. If a person has their own care plan or instructions from their care team, follow those instead of this site.

The Low helper

The Low helper is a memory aid for first aid. It is not a medical device and it does not measure, monitor or diagnose anything.

  • Its timer, sound, vibration, saved progress and offline mode depend on your phone and browser, and any of them can fail. Do not rely on the alarm alone: keep watching the person and the clock.
  • It does not show or dial an emergency number. You need to know and call the correct number for where you are.
  • It cannot tell how the person is doing. If they get worse, or you are in any doubt, call for medical help.

Examples are made up

Maya and Priya are invented people. Their stories, blood sugar readings and days are illustrations, not real records and not targets to copy. The phrase “about 180 health-related decisions a day” comes from a 2014 Stanford Medicine article and is an estimate.
