Type 1, explained

Insulin is the key that lets sugar into your cells.

In type 1 diabetes, the body stops making that key. Here's what that means, in about three minutes.

The pancreas makes insulin. Insulin unlocks your cells, sugar moves in, and you get energy.

So what is type 1 diabetes?

Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition. The body's immune system, which normally fights germs, mistakenly attacks the cells in the pancreas that make insulin.

Once those cells are gone, the body makes little or no insulin. Without it, sugar from food stays in the blood instead of fuelling the body. Over time, high blood sugar can harm the eyes, kidneys, nerves and heart, which is why it has to be managed every day.

Nobody knows exactly what triggers it. It isn't caused by eating too much sugar or by anything a person did, and it can't be prevented. It often starts in childhood or the teenage years, but it can appear at any age.

Type 1 isn't the same as type 2

Type 1

The body makes little or no insulin. It's autoimmune, often starts young, and always needs insulin.

Type 2

The body still makes insulin but doesn't respond to it well. It's far more common, linked to genes and lifestyle, and is often managed with diet, exercise and tablets.

Living with it

There's no cure yet, but type 1 is very manageable. Day to day, it comes down to three things.

  • Taking insulin

    Several injections a day with an insulin pen, or a small pump worn on the body that delivers it automatically.

  • Checking blood sugar

    With a finger-prick meter, or a sensor on the arm that reads sugar levels around the clock and sends them to a phone.

  • Balancing food and activity

    Matching insulin to meals and exercise. Too little insulin means high sugar; too much can cause a low, which is fixed quickly with juice or glucose tablets.

Signs to know

Type 1 can come on over a few weeks. Four early signs are easy to remember:

Toiletneeding to pee a lot
Thirstyalways feeling dry
Tiredwiped out for no reason
Thinnerlosing weight without trying

If you notice these, especially in a child, see a doctor the same day. A simple blood or urine test can check for it.

People with type 1 go to school, run marathons, raise families and do every job you can think of.

They just do it while quietly doing the work of a pancreas. A little understanding from the people around them goes a long way.
