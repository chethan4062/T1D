Below the surface

Friday night. Priya, 31, is out for dinner with friends. She was diagnosed with type 1 diabetes at 24. Nothing at this table would tell you.

Scroll to go under

Depth 1 · Under the sleeve

She's wearing two medical devices

A sensor on her arm reads her sugar level every few minutes. A pump at her waist delivers insulin through a thin tube. She wears them day and night and replaces them herself.

Depth 2 · In the bag

She never leaves home without a kit

A backup insulin pen in case the pump fails. A meter to double-check the sensor. Juice and glucose tablets for lows. Spares of everything. Forgetting it can mean turning around and going home.

Depth 3 · On the phone

Her phone is a medical monitor

The sensor sends a reading to her phone all day, and alarms don't wait for a good moment. This one arrived on Tuesday, five minutes before a meeting she was leading.

Depth 4 · In her head

Every meal starts with maths

Before the first bite tonight, Priya works through all of this, and nobody at the table notices. A Stanford Medicine article put it at about 180 health-related decisions a day.

Depth 5 · 3 am

It doesn't stop at night

3:07 am. An alarm says she's low. She walks to the kitchen, drinks juice, and waits fifteen minutes to check again. By 3:30 she's back in bed. At 9 she's at her desk, and nobody knows.

Back at the surface

Same table. Same Priya.

Now you know what's underneath. Most of type 1 is invisible, and the people who live with it do this work every day, on top of everything else.

What helps

You don't need to understand the maths. A few small things make life easier for someone with type 1.

  • Let them get on with it

    Checking a phone, pressing buttons on a pump or taking insulin at the table is ordinary. No need to look away or make it a moment.

  • Skip the food commentary

    “Should you be eating that?” never helps. They've already done the sums, probably twice.

  • Know what a low looks like

    If they seem shaky, sweaty or confused, offer juice and stay with them. Here's what to do.
